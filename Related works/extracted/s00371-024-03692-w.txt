Abstract
In recent years, the international population has surged which has led to increased pressure on food security. Plant diseases are
one of the most essential factors affecting food security. Plant diseases often manifest pathological features on plant leaves.
This paper combines deep learning with continual learning and federated learning, proposing a federated continual learning
comprehensive model based on the Swin Transformer model (SSPW224-LwF-3). Our datasets come from shared datasets and
integrate data from other datasets based on a ratio principle of 6:1:3 for the training set, validation set and test set (https://osf.
io/v4qfr/?view_only=38b53e39988c4e1e82363031a921f799). Experimental results show that the SSPW224 model achieves
an accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score of 97.20%, 95.25%, 94.85% and 94.71% on the test set. It is evident to ﬁnd out
that the SSPW224 model outperforms the other network models in the identiﬁcation and classiﬁcation of plant leaf diseases
from the data and visualization perspectives. The average accuracy of the SSPW224-LwF-3 model strategy combination
proposed in this study reaches the highest 47.06% among 18 combination strategies, with an accuracy of 49.41% and 33.81%
for old and new data. The overall performance of this model is optimal. Therefore, the proposed SSPW224-LwF-3 model
can achieve efﬁcient, sustainable, and distributed recognition and classiﬁcation of plant leaf diseases which provides a tool
for plant disease identiﬁcation in smart agriculture.
Keywords Leaf disease · Deep learning · Swin Transformer · Continual learning · Federated learning · t-SNE
1 Introduction
With the emergence of academic disciplines such as plant
pathology and microbiology, research on plant diseases is
gradually deepening. Leaf disease is one of the most common
types of plant disease, which is caused by a certain plant dis-
ease and results in some aberrant leaf traits such as yellowing
[1], black spots [2] or mosaic patterns [3] on the leaves. The
etiology of leaf diseases encompasses multifaceted factors,
such as fungal infection, bacterial infection, viral invasion
and other causes such as mosquito bites.
The ﬁrst etiological category is fungal leaf diseases [4],
which is caused by fungi such as powdery mildew, rust dis-
ease and anthracnose. Exempliﬁed by Diplocarpon Rosae

The third etiological category is viral leaf diseases [8],
which is caused by viral such as potato leafroll virus (PLRV)
and tobacco mosaic virus (TMV). These viruses can be trans-
mitted through insects or other diseased plants. We take
tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) [9] as an example. Illustratively,

synthesized virus particles gather inside the cells and form
elongated nanotube structures through self-assembly. This
can disrupt the cell membrane of host plant cells, perturbing
plant hormone regulation. Eventually, it elicits abnormal leaf
characteristics such as yellowing, curling and disease spots
on the leaves.
Leaf diseases may have a variety of effects on plants
because leaf disease can hinder the photosynthesis of most
plants to a certain extent. The ability to carry out photosyn-
thesis normally is the key to whether plants can grow and
survive normally. And leaf is the main place for plant photo-
synthesis. Leaf diseases can lead to a decrease in the number
of cells which can carry out photosynthesis in plant leaves
and a decrease in chlorophyll content in plant leaves. So, it
will lead to some abnormal symptoms such as leaf yellow-
ing and curling, which reduces the efﬁciency and effective
areaofplantphotosynthesis.Plantscannotsynthesizeenough
organic matter without enough photosynthesis, ultimately
leading to serious consequences such as slowing down plant
growth and lowering seed quality. If this malignant outcome
is reﬂected signiﬁcantly in the ﬁeld of grain crops, it will lead
to a substantial reduction in the yield of grain crops. The yield
of grain crops is of vital signiﬁcance to human survival and
development and the harmonious development of society.
Firstly, enough food is the key to food security and the basis
for human survival and development. Secondly, food produc-
tion is the economic pillar of many developing countries. The
identiﬁcation and classiﬁcation of plant leaf diseases are the
basis of studying and solving plant leaf diseases. Therefore,
it is very important to improve the accuracy of identifying
and classifying plant diseases.
In the early research of plant leaf diseases, manual fea-
ture extraction [10, 11] is a common method. Early research
on plant leaf diseases primarily relied on manual feature
extraction methods, such as visual observation and manual
measurement of leaf color, shape and size.
However, the method of manual feature extraction for
plant leaves has subjectivity, which is not sensitive enough to
subtle disease symptoms and has limitations. Additionally,
this method also has the disadvantages of being time-
consumingandrequiringalargeamountofwork.Itisdifﬁcult
for this method to cope with high-capacity datasets. With
the advancement of science and technology, people began
to pursue the task of identifying the characteristics of leaves
with high efﬁciency and high accuracy, so the use of machine
learning in artiﬁcial intelligence to identify and classify plant
leaf diseases came into being [12]. Prabhjot Kaur et al. used
logistic regression techniques to sample collected features
and employed a hybrid convolutional neural network to iden-
tify leaf disease, ultimately achieving an accuracy of 98.7%.
Panuwat Mekha et al. [13] used a random forest classiﬁca-
tion algorithm to identify and categorize a dataset of rice
leaf brown spot disease (BSR), ﬁnally achieving an accu-
racy of 69.44%. Ms. Deepika Chauhan et al. extensively
employed artiﬁcial intelligence methods, such as naive Bayes
(NB), decision trees (DT), K-nearest neighbors (K-NN),
support vector machine (SVM) and random forest (RF), to
detect crop diseases. Then they proposed a model which is
the most suitable for efﬁcient disease detection. The accu-
racy of the random forest model is the highest, at 80.68%
[14]. Muhammad Alyas Khan [15] proposed using gray-
level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) and Color Moments to
extract color and shape features of leaf segmentation regions.
Finally, they put these extracted features into a backpropaga-
tion neural network (BPNN) classiﬁer to detect and classify
walnut leaf diseases. The experimental result shows that the
detection accuracy of the proposed method is 95.3%. Mar-
janul Islam Tarik et al. [16] proposed an automated system
which is based on image processing and machine learning
for identifying and classifying potato leaf diseases. They
analyzed more than 2034 images of unhealthy potatoes and
healthy leaves from publicly accessible plant datasets. The
program achieved a prediction accuracy of 99.23% when
tested with 25% test data and 75% training data. Their result
indicates that the method of machine learning surpasses all
existing methods in the potato disease detection ﬁeld. Sunil
Harakannanavar et al. [17] used algorithms such as support
vector machine (SVM), convolutional neural network (CNN)
and K-nearest neighbors (K-NN) to extract tomato features
and classify them. The accuracy of the proposed model was
tested on diseased tomato samples by SVM, K-NN and CNN,
and the accuracy was 88%, 97% and 99.6%, respectively.
Muhammad Anwarul Azim et al. [18] used an extreme gra-
dient boosting decision tree ensemble method on UCI’s rice
leaf disease dataset, resulting in an 86.58% accuracy. Seyed
Mohamad Javidan et al. [19] achieved automatically sepa-
rated diseased areas from healthy parts of leaves by using
K-means clustering and extracted features in RGB, HSV and
Labcolormodels.HarikumarPallathadkaetal.[20]proposed
a framework based on machine learning for rice leaf disease
classiﬁcation and detection. SVM, naive Bayes and CNN are
used in this framework. PCA algorithm was also used for fea-
ture extraction. Sambasivam et al. [21] proposed the use of
category weights, SMOTE (synthetic minority oversampling

Deep learning is an end-to-end learning approach that
allows tasks to be learned directly from raw data through end-
to-end learning, and weights can be automatically adjusted
during the training process. So, it can reduce the need for
manual design processes. Compared to machine learning,
deep learning models can often achieve better performance
when dealing with large-scale and complex image data [22].
In the ﬁeld of plant leaf disease recognition, Ümit Atila et al.
proposed the framework of EfﬁcientNet deep learning for
plant leaf disease classiﬁcation, and they trained the model
on the PlantVillage dataset. The accuracy of EfﬁcientNet-
B5 and EfﬁcientNet-B4 models in the original and enhanced
datasets is 99.91% and 99.97%, respectively. Rakesh Chan-
dra Joshi et al. proposed a method named VirLeafNet based
on deep learning and achieved automatic virus infection
detection for Vigna mungo. All proposed models achieved
over 90% accuracy [6]. Andrew J. et al. [23] ﬁne-tuned
popular pre-trained models’ hyperparameters. They used the
PlantVillage dataset in experiments, and the DenseNet-121
model achieved a higher classiﬁcation accuracy of 99.81%.
B. Sai Reddy et al. [24] used convolutional neural net-
works (CNN) to train models with supervised pixel values,
achieving an accuracy of over 97%. Shivali Amit Wagle [25]
used some deep learning models such as AlexNet, VGG16,
GoogLeNet, MobileNetv2 and SqueezeNet to classify and
judge tomato plant leaves. Among them, the VGG16 model
has the best performance, with an accuracy of 99.17%. Arun
Malik et al. [26] proposed a mixed model combining VGG16
and MobileNet for sunﬂower disease identiﬁcation and clas-
siﬁcation. On the same dataset, this method outperformed
other algorithms with an accuracy of 89.2%. Hesham Tarek
et al. [27] investigated various deep convolutional neural net-
work (CNN) technologies such as ResNet50, InceptionV3,
AlexNet and MobileNetV1 to detecting and identifying
tomato leaf diseases. MobileNetV3 Large got the highest
accuracy of 99.81% among other deep learning models, with
a loss value of 0.0088. Yousef Methkal Abd Algani et al. [28]
proposed a deep learning algorithm called ant colony opti-
mization with convolutional neural network (ACO-CNN).
They studied the diagnostic effect of plant leaf diseases by
using ant colony optimization (ACO) and used a CNN clas-
siﬁer to extract image information features. Compared to
existing techniques, the proposed method performs better
in terms of accuracy. Marriam Nawaz et al. [29] proposed a
method used for classifying tomato plant leaf diseases which
is based on ResNet-34 Faster-RCNN. ResNet-34 and con-
volutional block attention module (CBAM) were introduced
as the feature extraction module for Faster-RCNN to extract
deep-level key points. They tested this model on the PlantVil-
lage Kaggle dataset and achieved an accuracy of 99.97%.
Waleed Albattah et al. used an improved CenterNet to detect
and classify various plant leaf diseases. In the experiment,
they used DenseNet-77 for deep-level key point extraction.
From this, it is evident that using deep learning methods to
identify and classify plant leaf diseases has overcome the
limitations of manually extracting image features compared
to traditional methods and machine learning methods. And
deep learning methods do not require manual intervention
[30]. This makes the models more adaptable and can han-
dle different types and degrees of diseases, which plays an
important role in preventing and managing plant diseases.
However, in the classiﬁcation process, each model has cer-
tain shortcomings that may not be applicable to some speciﬁc
datasets. Catastrophic forgetting [31–33] is a serious but nor-
mal phenomenon that occurs in the process of deep learning
when we solve practical problems. Catastrophic forgetting
refers to the phenomenon where a neural network forgets
previously learned tasks when learning new tasks. When a
neural network is designed to solve multiple tasks, its weight
parameters will be adjusted during the learning stage of new
tasks, causing the loss of previously acquired knowledge.
This phenomenon occurs because the weight parameters of
a neural network inevitably change when learning new tasks
to adapt to new inputs and objectives. The updates of these
weights may lead to a decline in the network’s performance
on previous tasks, because the new parameter settings may
no longer be suitable for the old tasks. Therefore, giving deep
learning models the ability for continuous learning is the top
priority in solving practical problems. Our research builds
upon existing models and incorporate the strategies for fed-
erated learning and continual learning. The method we built
gets more accuracy. And it is more efﬁcient and resilient to
the problem of existing catastrophic forgetting compared to
other methods. The main contributions of this study are as
follows:
• We have established a comprehensive and integrated
datasets of various plant diseases images for training neu-
ral networks which is capable of identifying multiple plant
diseases. We have also appropriately allocated the training
sets, validation sets and test sets.
• We have compared several advanced deep learning meth-
ods on the comprehensive datasets we proposed and
identiﬁed the model with the best performance on our
datasets. We have validated the neural network based on
the Swin Transformer architecture using multiple eval-
uation metrics for each classiﬁcation. We applied the
confusion matrix and t-SNE dimensionality reduction

2 Materials
2.1 Dataset
The dataset used in this paper is obtained from the Kag-
gle website. We chose a dataset named as Leaf Disease
Dataset (combination) which focuses on plant leaf diseases.
This dataset is based on many famous datasets such as the
Plantvillage dataset, Rice Leaf Disease dataset and Planti-
fyDr dataset. Upon our review, we found errors in the cassava
dataset in the original data, and the data for the apple cedar
rust disease category were insufﬁcient. Additionally, it was
difﬁcult to ﬁnd enough suitable image data online to supple-
ment this category. Therefore, we deleted the cassava dataset
and apple cedar rust from this dataset. The basic quantity
information of the dataset is shown in Table 1. Our dataset is
divided into training sets, testing sets and validation sets. All
of the pictures are well organized in the training directory,
testingdirectoryandvalidationdirectorywithintheirrelevant
class name folders. We optimized the quantity distribution
of picture data in the training sets, validation sets and testing
sets based on the original dataset. The ﬁnal ratio of picture
data for each disease subclasses (including “healthy”) in the
training sets, validation sets and testing sets is 6:1:3. Then,
to avoid overﬁtting of the model, we increased the number of
image data of the original dataset according to the set ratio.
We have endeavored to ensure an ample number of samples
in the test set, with the majority of test set samples exceeding
200 in quantity. The dataset includes 12 plant classes, fur-
ther subdivided into 38 plant disease subclasses (including
“healthy”). They are all standardized to 256 × 256 size and
RGB color scheme.
We take the ﬁve leaf diseases of tomatoes as an exam-
ple. Figure 1 shows the illustrations of healthy tomato leaves
and other leaves who suffer from these ﬁve diseases: tomato
mosaic virus, tomato yellow leaf curl virus, bacterial spot,
early blindness and leaf mold.
It is not difﬁcult to see that the leaf of healthy tomato is
uniformly green, with relatively few lobes and no signiﬁ-
cant differences in size between lobes. The leaf margins are
smooth. Tomato leaves infected with tomato mosaic virus
(TMV) will show color differences on the surface. It is
obvious that there are variations in green color intensity at
different positions on the leaves. There will be a slight curva-
ture phenomenon at the leaf margin. Tomato leaves infected
with tomato yellow leaf curl virus will exhibit severe curva-
ture of the leaf margins and yellowing of the leaves. Tomato
leaves infected with bacterial spot will show irregular dark
brown spots which will continuously spread. Tomato leaves
infected with early blight will have irregular black–brown
spots and yellowing around the spots, too. And there will be
a slight curvature phenomenon at the leaf margin. Tomato
leaves infected with leaf mold will show a relatively concen-
trated yellowing phenomenon.
2.2 Data preprocessing
In order to enhance the diversity of data, the ability of gen-
eralization and performance of the model, it is necessary to
preprocess the experimental data. We uniformly scale the
images in the dataset to 224 × 224 pixels and apply blur
processing to them. Then we normalize the image data. We
also added salt-and-pepper noise and Gaussian noise to the
images in the dataset to simulate random interference and
scene changes in our real life. In order to improve the qual-
ity of the dataset, we perform data augmentation on the
images in the training set, including random rotation, hor-
izontal ﬂipping, random cropping and randomly changing
the brightness, contrast, saturation and hue attributes of the
images. Taking an image from Apple Scale as an example,
the following are the images of a plant leaf affected by Apple
Scale disease that has undergone cropping, rotation, horizon-
tal ﬂipping, changing image attributes, blurring [34–36] and
adding salt-and-pepper noise [37] or Gaussian noise [38].
