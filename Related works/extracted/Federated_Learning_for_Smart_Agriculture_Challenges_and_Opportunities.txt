Abstract—FL is a machine learning approach that allows
knowledge sharing with privacy maintenance and cost reduction.
FL has the potential to revolutionize the smart agriculture sector
by enabling farmers to train and deploy machine learning models
on their own devices, without the need to share their data with
a central server. In this article, we review the applications of
FL in smart agriculture, such as disease diagnosis and severity
assessment, the Internet of Agricultural Things (IoAT), and yield
forecasting. We also highlight the challenges and opportunities
of using FL for smart agriculture.
Index Terms—Smart Agriculture, Federated Learning, Dis-
tributed Learning, Disease Detection, Severity Assessment, Yield
Forecasting.
I. INTRODUCTION
Smart agriculture refers to smart and efficient agriculture
with the use of technologies such as the Internet of Things
(IoT), sensors, location systems, robots, and artificial intel-
ligence in farming practices [1]. The goal of smart agricul-
ture is to improve the cost effectiveness and sustainability
of agriculture while improving crop yields [2] . Machine
learning (ML) techniques are used to predict crop yield,
early detection of crop diseases, estimation of crop damage
by weather parameters such as atmospheric temperature and
humidity, assessment of disease severity, and detection of
soil nutrition, water quality management, crop monitoring,
livestock monitoring, etc. [1]. Researchers have developed
various solutions based on artificial neural networks (ANN) to
simplify agricultural tasks. In general, farm data is obtained
from various sources such as IoT sensors, organizational data
archives, and users and are then transferred to cloud servers
where machine learning models are trained. This approach has
a significant limitation, especially when the training data set
contains sensitive information, as it poses security threats, and
this gives rise to concerns about the privacy of the data [3].
In 2016, Google introduced FL, an emerging approach that
addresses this limitation by allowing knowledge sharing while
maintaining privacy and reducing costs [4]. FL allows devices
to collectively learn a shared model while keeping learning
data stored on devices, thus separating machine learning
functionality from the need for centralized data storage [5].
FL is also beneficial in resource-constrained situations, such
as mobile devices, IoT devices, or networks with restricted
bandwidth, because it tries to be both effective and scalable
[6]. Figure 1 shows the general applications of FL in agricul-
ture, advantages, challenges and limitations of using FL.
FL in smart agriculture has revolutionized data storage and
analysis in the field. Using the power of distributed computing,
FL allows researchers to train ML models using data from
multiple sources, such as sensors, drones, and IoT devices,
without the need to centralize the data. This decentralized
approach not only ensures data privacy and security, but also
enables real-time decision-making and optimization of farming
practices. With FL, farmers can now benefit from the collec-
tive intelligence of a network of devices, resulting in more
accurate predictions, improved crop yields, and sustainable
agricultural practices without compromising data privacy [7].
This innovative technology has the potential to transform the
agriculture industry by enabling efficient resource allocation,
reducing environmental impact, and, ultimately, ensuring food
security for a growing global population.

A. Advantages of FL in smart agriculture
The utilization of ML in the field of agriculture offers
numerous benefits, such as the ability to detect crop diseases at
an early stage, detect intrusions, manage crops, and assess the
situation and severity of various issues. However, the imple-
mentation of FL introduces additional advantages over ML,
such as the preservation of privacy for crop data, reduction
in communication costs, scalability, increased data diversity
and many more [7]. Some of the advantages of FL in smart
agriculture are described in the following subsections.
1) Privacy Preservation: In the traditional ML approaches,
there is a concern about privacy as data is exchanged between
clients and the server. However, FL addresses this issue by
storing crop data on the device and only transmitting model
updates to the central server. By keeping the data on the
device, FL ensures the protection of raw data, reduces the
chances of data leakage, and ensures secure operations. The FL
model gathers crop data and stores it exclusively at the edge,
minimizing the risk of data leakage and ensuring the security
of operations. Consequently, the data remains preserved.
2) Distributed Processing: The process of collecting crop
data and training a model requires significant computational
resources and time. However, FL offers a solution by enabling
distributed processing, where training occurs simultaneously
on multiple edge nodes. This approach helps to save compu-
tational resources and reduce the time required for training.
3) Decentralized Data Storage: In contrast to the tradi-
tional ML approach, FL enables crop data to be stored in
a distributed manner across multiple devices or servers rather
than being centralized in a single location. This decentralized
data storage offers several advantages, including addressing
privacy and security concerns. Since the data remains on the
local devices or servers and is not transmitted to a central
server, the risk of data breaches or unauthorized access to
sensitive information is reduced. Additionally, FL leads to
improved scalability and efficiency.
4) Reduced Communication Overheads: If the clients or
local servers are located far away from the centralized server,
transferring data from the local servers to the central server
becomes challenging. This issue may arise if the local servers
are in areas without communication lines or if there are fre-
quent disruptions in the communication lines. Consequently,
transferring data to the central server becomes problematic. To
address this, decentralized data storage approach can be used,
where the model trains at the local servers and then transmits
the updated weights to the central server. This decentralized
approach can significantly reduce the cost of data transmission.
Additionally, to minimize upload times, the communication
between the clients and the central server is kept to a minimum
by only exchanging model updates, regardless of the size
of the dataset. This approach helps to greatly reduce the
communication costs involved.
5) Improved Security: The security of crop data is guaran-
teed as it is exclusively in the possession of the farmers.
6) Energy Efficient: Significant computational power is
required when training the model on a central server. In FL,
models are trained using smaller datasets, which results in
lower energy and power consumption compared to traditional

7) Continuous Learning: In certain cases, the data that
is received is in analog form rather than being discrete.
Continuous learning facilitates the process of learning from the

changes and enhancing real-time performance.
8) Collaborative Learning: The approach known as collab-
orative learning involves training multiple classifier heads of
the same network on the same training data simultaneously.
This is done to enhance generalization and increase resistance
to label noise, all without incurring any additional inference
cost. Another term used to describe this approach is FL, as it
involves using multiple sources to individually train the model
and then combining them into a single model. This helps to
improve accuracy and decrease the training time required for
the model.
9) Scalability: Federated learning enables the storage of
datasets in various locations and the concurrent training of
models. This speeds up the deployment of AI and IoT models
and enhances operational scalability spatially.
10) Improved Data Diversity: In the agricultural sector,
FL algorithms are utilized to train models using data from
various crops situated in different geographical regions. This
approach leads to the creation of comprehensive models as
the dataset consists of crops with diverse characteristics such
as humidity, pest infestation, heavy metal content, and crop
fertility. Consequently, the models developed through this
method exhibit enhanced capacity to effectively handle new
data, accommodate variations, and mitigate bias.
II. LITERATURE REVIEW
A. Disease Classification
Mehta et al. [9] used a lightweight convolutional neural
network model to identify and categorize mango leaf diseases.
The effectiveness of this lightweight model was evaluated in
four different clients and five disease categories, including
healthy, anthracnose, powdery mildew, leaf spot, and leaf curl.
The researchers achieved a detection accuracy of 98% for these
particular diseases. Table I presents a comparative analysis of
existing studies.
In a study by Aggarwal et al. [6], the EfficientNetB3 model
was used to detect rice diseases. The data set consisted of
5,932 images from rice fields in Odisha, India, as well as
an agricultural and insect pest image database. The model
achieved an accuracy of 99.79% for IID data and 97.48% for
non-IID data.
The classification of pear disease was performed by Dock-
endorf et al. [17]. The authors integrated FL for training the
various CNNs in TinyML devices and benchmarked different
CNNs.
B. Disease Severity Assessment
Mehta et al. [11] used MobileNet to identify apple leaf
disease and assess its severity levels. They examined the
effectiveness of the model in four different categories of
severity: healthy, mild, moderate, and severe. The focus was on
two classes of specific diseases, namely apple scab, marsonina
coronoria, and Alternaria leaf blotch. The researchers achieved
a remarkable accuracy of 99.2% in detecting these diseases.

D. UAV for Pest Detection
Khan et al. [16] employed FL-based unmanned aerial ve-
hicles (UAVs) to classify various pests. They used an Effi-
cientNet B03 for classification and detection purposes and
compared the results with other models such as ANN, SVM,
KNN and Naive Bayes. The CNN model demonstrated the
highest accuracy of 99.55%.
Deng et al. [12] utilized a faster R-CNN to detect orchard
pests. They employed the Online Hard Example Mining
(OHEM) network training method to improve detection ac-
curacy. The experimental findings indicated that the improved
model achieved an accuracy of 90.27% for the detection of
multiple pests.
E. Intrusion Detection System
Man et al [14] proposed an intelligent intrusion detection
system using edge-assisted IoT. They utilized the Federated
Attention Convolution Neural Network (FedACNN) and eval-
uated their model using the NSL-KDD dataset. The model
achieved an accuracy of 99.76%. Compared to the Federated
Average (FedAVG) model, the number of communication
rounds was reduced to 50%.
Friha et al. [4] conducted a study on the performance of
the Intrusion Detection System (IDS) using three different
data sets. CSE-CIC-IDS2018, MQTTset, and InSDN. They
experimented with three deep learning classifiers: deep neu-
ral networks (DNN), recurrent neural networks (RNN), and
convolutional neural networks (CNN). The FL-based Intrusion
Detection System (FELIDS) using a DNN model achieved an
accuracy of 98.54% with the dataset collected from InSDN,
outperforming other systems..
Zainuddin et al. [15] developed and evaluated the per-
formance of a lightweight DNN model called IDSFedNet
on the InSDN and Edge-IIoTset datasets. Their framework
utilized the Chi-square and Pearson correlation coefficients to
reduce the complexity of the model and improve performance.
The model outperformed existing FL-based intrusion detection
models, achieving accuracies of 90.83% for the InSDN dataset
and 95.41% for the Edge-IIoTset dataset.
F. Yield Forecasting
Manoj et al. [13] used a deep residual network to predict
the soybean yield. They evaluated the performance of this
model by comparing it with other learning models, namely
LASSO, Random Forest, and Resnet-28. The results obtained
demonstrated an accuracy of 99.95%.
G. Crop Risk Analysis
Yu et al. [18] introduced a risk-entropy model that uses
FL. This model assesses the risk associated with crops, con-
sidering various factors such as policy, economic conditions,
knowledge and information, infrastructure, cultural aspects,
access to food, climate conditions, and social challenges. This
model quantitatively describes the level of risk for agricultural
products. They tested this model using a dataset of 90,510
agricultural product samples from 2015 to 2019. To implement
this model in smart agriculture, three components are neces-
sary: parties, a manager (server), and a framework. Parties
refer to the data owners who possess the data, while the
manager is a device that aggregates local models, performs the
required computations, and sends back the updated model. To
facilitate computation, privacy mechanisms, scheduling, and
frameworks are essential.
TOOLS
There are several tools available to implement FL. Some
popular FL tools include TensorFlow Federated (TFF) [19],
PySyft [20], IBM Federated Learning Framework [21], [22],
and Fate framework [23]. These tools provide the libraries
necessary to enable FL in various agricultural applications.

H. Matlab
Matlab serves as a valuable tool for analyzing and designing
systems and products. The use of Matlab for model simula-
tion is widespread among researchers [24], [25]. It offers a
wide range of libraries for the execution and construction of
machine learning algorithms and neural networks. Matlab is
compatible with most models.
I. TensorFlow Federated(TFF)
Google’s TensorFlow offers an API that allows users to
develop and customize their own models for experimentation.
TensorFlow Federated (TFF) is an open source framework that
extends Keras models to enable computation on vertically and
horizontally distributed data. With TFF, developers can apply
FL algorithms to their models and data. Keras, on the other
hand, provides a user-friendly and efficient interface to solve
machine learning problems.
J. Fate
The framework Fate [26] enables secure computation
through the use of homomorphic encryption and multiparty
computation, making it widely recognized and popular. It
offers support for a range of machine learning algorithms,
including logistic regression, tree-based algorithms, and deep
learning techniques such as deep learning neural networks and
transfer learning. Over time, Fate has evolved into various
branches, each serving different purposes. These branches
provide a wide array of services, with FATE-cloud offering
industrial-level Federated Learning cloud services, the Eggroll
framework facilitating the implementation of machine learning
models, and the FATE-LLM framework specifically supporting
FL for large language models (LLMs).
K. IBM Federated learning
IBM Federated Learning, which was created by IBM Tech-
nologies [27], includes supervised, unsupervised, and rein-
forcement learning. It can work with any neural network
topology that is compatible with Keras, PyTorch, and Ten-
sorFlow. Furthermore, it supports Decision Tree ID3, linear
classifiers, linear regression, linear SVM, ridge regression,
k-means clustering, and Nave Bayes. It also supports deep
reinforcement learning models such as DQN, DPPG, PDO,
and others.
L. Flower Framework
Researchers widely adopt the Flower framework because
of its versatility. It offers both an Android SDK and an iOS
SDK, making it compatible with a wide range of devices.
Additionally, Flower can seamlessly integrate with various
other frameworks, including PyTorch, TensorFlow, Hugging
Face Transformers, Scikit-Learn, and more.
M. Pysyft
PySyft is a widely adopted framework that employs various
techniques, such as federated learning, differential privacy, and
encrypted computation, to separate private data from model
training. Currently, it exclusively supports the deep learning
libraries PyTorch and TensorFlow. However, starting with
version 0.7, it will also offer a TensorFlow Federated API.

III. DISCUSSIONS
FL enables the devices to collaboratively learn from the
model while keeping the data to themselves, unlike traditional
learning, where the data is transferred to the central server. The
main reason for the need for FL is farmers’ concern about the
privacy of their crop data. According to the survey conducted
by the American Farm Bureau Federation, 77% of farmers are
concerned about the privacy of their data [36].
We need hardware and software elements to implement Fed-
erated learning. In the hardware segment, there are three essen-
tial components: end devices, training devices, and databases.
Environmental sensors are responsible for monitoring vari-
ous factors such as soil conditions, humidity, plant health,
and temperature. Soil sensors are specifically designed to
monitor pH and metal concentration. While sensors collect
data, devices process and analyze it based on criteria such
as the number of features and their complexity. To ensure
privacy, additional techniques such as privacy encoding and
homomorphic encryption can be used.
For low-complexity models with simple characteristics,
microcontrollers such as Arduino, Raspberry Pi, and mobile
phones can be utilized. However, clients involved in decen-
tralized federated learning require more computing power to
communicate and share model parameters without relying on a
central server. Training on mobile phones can be challenging.
Since the model is trained locally, it can be stored on mobile
phones or any SSDs connected to microcontrollers.
When it comes to software requirements, we require a
framework that can run our model, manage the aggregation
process, and handle a large number of devices, ranging from
thousands to millions. We have previously discussed several
frameworks in the previous sections.
Although Federated Learning shows great potential, there
exist various challenges and limitations in its implementation,
as outlined in the following sections.
A. Communication
Areas with limited network bandwidth and heterogeneous
networks, such as remote rural areas where agriculture is the
main industry, are expected to experience increased communi-
cation costs, an increased number of communication rounds,
and reduced latency. However, there is a lack of techniques
that efficiently support communication by reducing the total
number of communication rounds and operating effectively in
areas of high bandwidth [37].
