Abstract—The article addresses the problem of crop produc-
tion in the face of biotic and abiotic factors. Highlights the
importance of integrated management strategies to optimize
resources and increase crop resilience. Multispectral images cap-
tured by an Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) are used to detect
stress in crops. The article presents the K-means segmentation
method and highlights its superiority over the Otsu threshold
method for efficient segmentation. The relevance of vegetation
indices and machine learning in stress assessment is emphasized.
The proposed methodology includes data preprocessing, crop
detection and stress identification, integrating various techniques.
Experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of this imple-
mentation in differentiating healthy and stressed crop regions.
Taken together, the study contributes to precision agriculture
by combining advanced technologies and innovative techniques
for more accurate stress detection and more efficient crop
management.
Index Terms—Stress detection, segmentation, multispectral
imaging, remote sensing, satellite networks
I. INTRODUCTION
Crops are essential for food security and economic develop-
ment in many countries. However, various biotic and abiotic
factors threaten its production, which can cause plant stress
and negatively affect its growth, development, and yield [1].
Among these factors are water deficit, pests, diseases, weeds,
salinity, temperature, and radiation, which can reduce the
quality and quantity of agricultural products [2]. To face these
challenges is necessary to implement integrated management
strategies that allow optimizing the use of resources, improving
the efficiency and sustainability of production, and increasing
the resilience of crops [3]–[5]. One of these strategies is
the early and accurate detection of stress in crops, which
makes it possible to identify problems, diagnose the causes,
assess the impact and apply corrective measures [6]. Detecting
stress in crops requires the use of advanced technologies
that provide detailed and precise information on the state of
plants at a spatial and temporal level. Various works have
demonstrated the effectiveness of spectral data processing,
computer vision, and machine learning for this purpose [7]–
[10]. These techniques make it possible to extract relevant
information from images captured by various sensors, such
as RGB, hyperspectral, multispectral, or thermal cameras, and
classify plants according to their health or stress [11]–[13].
In [7], a method based on self-organizing hierarchical classi-
fiers was proposed to detect biotic and abiotic stress in wheat
and barley crops using hyperspectral imaging. The applications
of machine learning for stress phenotyping in plants using
different data types, such as spectral, morphological, thermal,
or fluorescence, were reviewed in [8]. In [9], the use of
deep learning for stress imaging in plants was discussed,
highlighting its advantages, challenges, and possible future
directions. Finally, in
[10], an overview of the trends and
prospects of computer vision technology for stress and disease
detection in precision agriculture was given, covering different
types of crops, leaves, fruits, and vegetables.
Multispectral images are those that capture information in an
interval that comprises from 3 to 15 spectral bands, with an
approximate width of 100 nm. They are a powerful tool to
obtain a more complete and detailed vision, and they capture
data within wavelength ranges across the electromagnetic
spectrum. These ranges can include visible light and other
frequencies such as infrared, revealing additional information
about its physical and chemical properties [14].
From these data, it is possible to extract characteristics such
as vegetation indices that have shown a high correlation with
the physiological and sanitary state of the plants [7]. Each of
these indices has its advantages and limitations, depending on
the type of crop, plant cover, soil moisture, atmosphere, and
the sensor used.
The different spectral resolutions of the sensors that obtain
these images could significantly affect these vegetation indices.

Computer vision is a branch of artificial intelligence respon-

a valuable tool for identifying, analyzing, and diagnosing
the different elements present in crop images [10]. Machine

In addition, in order to detect stress in crops, it is necessary
to identify the area occupied by plants in multispectral images
previously. This could be achieved using segmentation tech-
niques, which consist of dividing images into homogeneous
regions based on criteria such as color, texture, or shape [15].
Segmentation allows separating the crop from the background
(soil, water, clouds). This facilitates the calculation of indica-
tors such as vegetation cover or potential yield [17].
In the previous work [18], we proposed an analytical method to
determine stress in sugarcane crops from multispectral images.
One of the most critical phases of this study was the detection
of the crop of interest, for which a segmentation method
based on the threshold was used, which, although it obtained
acceptable results, could be verified that it was not the best. In
this work, we propose to evaluate and compare the efficiency
of detecting the crop of interest and its relationship with the
detection of stress.

II. SYSTEM DESCRIPTION
Figure 1 describes a satellite Internet system for crop
monitoring and analysis that uses advanced technologies for
precision agriculture. The system includes the components
required for its implementation.
The Farm requires a multispectral camera, a georeferencing
Fig. 1. Satellite Internet System for Crop Monitoring and Analysis
device, an Drone, a router, and a Starlink antenna. The multi-
spectral camera is a camera that captures images at different
wavelengths of the electromagnetic spectrum and is used to
detect the state of health, water stress, biomass, or chlorophyll
in plants. The drone flies over the crop and takes images
with a multispectral camera, labeling them with positioning
information and the flight plan. These data are sent over the
internet using a router that is the one that distributes the
signal to the other devices on the farm. The Starlink antenna
connects to the nearest Starlink satellite and provides internet
by sending and receiving data via radio waves.
The Link section is the means of transporting data between
the farm and the data center. It includes the Starlink satellite
network and the Gateway Earth Station. Starlink satellites
are small satellites that orbit the Earth at an altitude of
about 550 km and that communicate with each other using
optical lasers. Each satellite has four dish-shaped antennas
that allow it to dynamically point at users and other satellites.
Starlink satellites form a mesh network that guarantees global
coverage and low latency. The Starlink ground station is a
facility that is responsible for sending and receiving data
from Starlink satellites and connecting them to the terrestrial
Internet network.
The Processing section represents the data center where the
information received is processed and made available to end
users. It includes a processing server and a database. The data
collected by the camera and the processed data are stored in the
database. The server processes the data stored in the database
and applies artificial intelligence algorithms to extract useful
information about the crop.
The Dashboards section represents the devices customers use
to monitor the farm using pre-configured panels that connect
to the database via the internet. The panels are graphical inter-
faces that show the information processed by the server about
the crop, such as maps, graphs, alerts, or recommendations.
III. METHODOLOGY
Figure 2 shows the flowchart for detecting crop stress using
multispectral images. The process begins with obtaining multi-
spectral images produced by an unmanned aerial vehicle on the
crop under analysis under favorable atmospheric conditions.
Then comes a pre-processing stage in which vegetation indices
are aligned, corrected and calculated for subsequent analysis.
In the next stage, the objective is to detect or separate the crop
from the background, for which different techniques such as
Thresholding and Clustering can be used. Finally, the stress is
detected with the calculated parameters.
A. Data Pre-Processing
The images obtained by the Unmanned Aerial Vehicle
(UAV) present geometric errors due to perspective, camera tilt,
lens distortion, drone movement, height variation, insufficient
overlap, etc. These errors affect the quality and accuracy
of image analysis. To solve these problems, it is necessary
to create an orthomosaic, a continuous, homogeneous, and
rectified image of the crop. An orthomosaic of each band must
be constructed [6].

The Vegetation Index (VI) is a parameter that is calculated
from the reflectance of light at different wavelengths, which
varies according to the amount and state of the vegetation. The
vegetation indices are obtained through mathematics between
the spectral bands and are useful to differentiate the images
according to the physiological and growth parameters of the
plants. There are several types of vegetation indices, such as
the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), the Soil
Adjusted Vegetation Index (SAVI), the Enhanced Vegetation
Index (EVI2), and the Normalized Difference Green Vegeta-

C. Stress Detection
A simple method to detect the stressed crop is to apply
the logical AND operation to combine the segmentation of
the near infrared (NIR) band, which indicates the presence of
the crop, with each binary mask obtained by the calculated
vegetation index. For example, in [27] they identify a healthy
crop for NDVI with values greater than 0.39.
Another way to detect stress is using machine learning. For
this, it is necessary to have a reference of labels of stressed
or healthy plants, which ideally are obtained and verified by
experts. It is also required to have a series of characteristics
such as spectral bands, vegetation indices, and characterization
of texture or color. There are different supervised classification
algorithms that learn these labels and allow generalization to
new cultures [7].
IV. RESULTS
This study utilized 155 multispectral images from the NIR,
RED, GREEN, and RED EDGE bands, acquired through the
3DR RTF X8+ UAV and Parrot Sequoia 3.98 mm Multi-
spectral Camera. These images were taken at an average
height of 38.3 meters over an experimental sugarcane field
at (ETICA) [18]. Agisoft Metashape software was employed
to create georeferenced orthomosaics for each band, merging
images from diverse angles. This facilitated subsequent analy-
sis of the sugarcane crop, aiding in detecting stress and health.
To assess the health status of the vegetation, different indices
were calculated using the spectral bands of the multispectral
image. These indices are numerical measurements that relate
the different bands, these are sensitive to the photosynthetic ac-
tivity and the chlorophyll content of the plants. The calculated
indices were NDVI, SAVI, GNDVI and EVI2, which have
different properties and advantages depending on the terrain
conditions and vegetation cover.

V. CONCLUSIONS
In this study, crop stress detection was explored using
advanced technologies and innovative approaches. The imple-
mentation of a satellite and Internet-based monitoring system
made it possible to effectively evaluate the health of the crops.
A key contribution was the introduction and evaluation of the
K-means segmentation method to identify areas of interest
in multispectral images. Compared to the threshold method
Otsu, K-means demonstrated more accurate segmentation.
Furthermore, the importance of vegetation indices and the
integration of machine learning in crop stress assessment
was highlighted. This study advances precision agriculture by
combining emerging technologies and innovative methods to
improve crop stress detection and management.
