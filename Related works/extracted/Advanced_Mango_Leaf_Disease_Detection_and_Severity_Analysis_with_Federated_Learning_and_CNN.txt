Abstract— Worldwide mango production is threatened by 
mango leaf diseases, resulting in considerable financial losses. 
Effective illness management and reduction depend on early 
identification and severity level categorization. This research 
provides a brand-new FL-CNN model for categorizing mango 
leaf diseases into four severity categories. The FL-CNN model 
allows training to be distributed across several clients without 
compromising data privacy. The suggested model consistently 
outperforms all clients in identifying the four severity levels of 
mango leaf diseases. The model's efficiency across customers 
with various data distributions is shown using macro, 
weighted, and micro-averaging techniques. Client 4 had the 
greatest macro average (96.11), weighted average (96.09), and
micro average (96.08) values, while Client 1 had the lowest 
macro average (93.92), weighted average (93.96), and micro 
average (93.96) values. These findings show that the FL-CNN
model successfully recognizes and categorizes various degrees 
of mango leaf disease severity, making it an invaluable tool for 
practical agricultural applications. Utilizing federated learning 
provides data privacy while offering a scalable and safe 
method for managing plant diseases. Additionally, federated 
learning preserves data privacy and lessens the need for data 
centralization, offering a solid foundation for cooperation 
between many stakeholders. Future studies should focus on 
enhancing the model's effectiveness and accuracy and 
investigating whether it can predict additional plant diseases 
and agricultural situations. 
Keywords: Mango leaf diseases, Convolutional neural 
networks (CNNs), Federated learning, Disease classification, 
Severity levels. 
I.
INTRODUCTION
A tropical fruit tree with high economic and nutritional 
value, the mango (Mangifera indica L.) is extensively grown 
around the globe. However, several diseases, especially those 
that damage the leaves, may substantially impact the 
production and quality of mango harvests. These illnesses, 
which are brought on by pathogens like fungus, bacteria, and 
viruses, may significantly reduce crop yields and jeopardize 
the trees' health. To enact efficient control measures and 
maintain a sustainable agricultural system, timely diagnosis 
and precise categorization of mango leaf diseases and their 
severity levels are crucial. Four separate phases of mango 
leaf disease severity may be identified: (1) beginning, (2) 
mild, (3) moderate, and (4) severe. Each stage shows the 
disease's development and the severity of the leaf damage. It 
is essential to accurately classify these severity levels to 
choose the best management practices and lessen the effect 
on the mango crop. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) 
have been developed due to recent developments in 
computer vision and deep learning for various picture 
categorization applications, including plant disease detection. 
CNNs are a potential method for identifying illnesses in 
mango leaves because of their excellent accuracy and 
efficiency in detecting plant diseases. To train these models, 
a considerable quantity of data must be gathered and stored 
in a centralized repository, which might generate privacy and 
security issues, particularly when the data is sensitive or 
belongs to several parties. Federated learning is a distributed 
machine learning method that permits the localization of data 
while allowing the training of common models across several 
devices. CNN models [1] may be taught without needing a 
central repository for data storage by using federated 
learning, protecting data privacy and lowering data 
transmission costs. There are four key goals for this study. 
To further future research on mango leaf disease detection 
and classification, our first goal is to create a complete 
collection of annotated photos of mango leaves with disease 
kind and severity level. A unique Federated Learning-based 
Convolutional Neural Network (FL-CNN) [2] architecture 
will be created to categorize mango leaf diseases into four 
severity levels. Third, we will assess the suggested FL-CNN 
model's performance in contrast to conventional CNNs and 
other cutting-edge techniques, proving its potency in 
categorizing mango leaf illnesses and their severity levels. 
We will finally address possible difficulties, constraints, and 
future research prospects to further increase the precision and 
effectiveness of mango leaf disease classification using 
federated learning-based CNNs. This study suggests an FL-
CNN—a convolutional neural network based on federated 
learning—to categorize mango leaf illnesses into four 
severity categories. The following are the primary 
contributions of this study paper:
Creation of a comprehensive collection of photos of 
mango leaves tagged with information about the disease's 
kind and severity level. 
Design and implement a unique FL-CNN architecture 
emphasizing protecting data privacy and minimizing 
communication

A thorough analysis of the suggested FL-CNN model's 
performance compared to conventional CNNs and other 
cutting-edge techniques shows how well it can categorize 
mango leaf diseases and their severity levels.

LITERATURE REVIEW
The article describes how mango leaf diseases may 
reduce mango quality and productivity and how it might be 
challenging to diagnose them using conventional techniques 
effectively. Mango leaf illnesses have been classified by 
researchers using computer-aided and machine-learning 
techniques. However, these methods have certain drawbacks 
owing to increasing feature dimensionality, overfitting, 
computational 
complexity, 
and 
a 
lack 
of 
feature 
characteristics. The authors suggest a new mango leaf 
disease classification framework with four stages: data 
preparation, feature selection, learning and classification, and 
performance assessment. This framework aims to overcome 
these problems. To avoid overfitting and enhancing 
generalization, they chose 380 photos from the categories of 
healthy and ill mango leaves. They used a convolutional 
neural network with crossover-based levy flight distribution 
to improve feature selection. For the learning stage, they 
used a pre-trained MobileNetV2 model. For the classification 
step, they used a support vector machine. Comparing the 
experimental findings to other cutting-edge techniques, they 
showed greater categorization performance [3]. This research 
compared the efficacy of fuzzy logic and convolutional 
neural networks (CNN) for diagnosing mango leaf illnesses. 
The researchers utilised ten photos from the Seed Buzz 
website as a dataset to train and test the prediction model in 
MATLAB. The accuracy of the CNN and Fuzzy models 
were compared statistically using the SPSS programme. The 
fuzzy mean method had an accuracy of 93.5% and a 
significance value of 0.038 for accuracy and 0.073 for 
sensitivity, while the CNN-based system had a high accuracy 
of 95.2%. According to the study, the CNN-based model is 
more accurate than the Fuzzy Logic method in spotting 
mango leaf illnesses [4]. This study proposes a Brightness

work well in precisely and quickly identifying mango leaf 
illnesses [5]. This article focuses on discovering and 
identifying biotic stress factors, such as fungus and bacteria 
in mango leaves, which may significantly affect the crop's 
output. The impact of these pressures on the mango crop 
might be lessened by early diagnosis and treatment of these 
issues. The suggested method entails two steps: first, the area

98.12%, the suggested method fared better than previous

B. Model Aggregation  
The 
suggested 
strategy 
attempts 
to 
reduce 
communication overhead and preserve data privacy 
associated with typical centralised deep learning algorithms 
while developing an accurate and effective FL-CNN model 
for categorising mango leaf diseases into four severity levels. 
The model can be trained on a large-scale, heterogeneous 
dataset using federated learning without data owners 
disclosing their raw data. This makes it a safer and more 
privacy-preserving method for classifying plant diseases. The 
suggested FL-CNN model [14] will include several layers to
extract characteristics from the input photos and categorise 
them into four severity levels. A softmax activation function 
and Rectified Linear Units (ReLU) as the activation function 
for additional layers are also included in the model 
architecture, including 3 convolutional layers and 2 max-
pooling 2 dense layers, and 2 fully linked convolutional 
layers. The resolution of the supplied photos will always be

C. Federated Learning CNN Model 
Convolutional, pooling and fully connected layers will all 
be included in the proposed FL-CNN model. Convolutional 
layers will be in charge of removing features from the input 
photos, pooling layers will be in charge of shrinking the 
spatial dimensions, and fully connected layers will be in 
charge of categorizing the data. Dropout layers and batch 
normalization will also be used to enhance the model's 
functionality and avoid overfitting. An appropriate loss 
function, such as categorical cross-entropy, often used for 
multi-class classification problems, will be utilized to train 
the model. The optimizer will be selected to reduce the loss 
function and update the model weights during training. 
Examples of optimizers are Adam or RMSprop. The learning 
rate and other hyperparameters will be adjusted using a 
validation set to get the best model performance [15]. Data
owners (clients) using federated learning may participate in 
training without disclosing their information to a central 
server. A subset of clients will be randomly chosen 
throughout each training cycle to ensure privacy and 
guarantee a varied representation of the full dataset. Selected
customers will use their data to train the FL-CNN model 
locally on their smartphones. Each client will complete many 
training epochs to update the model weights, and the locally 
changed weights will be relayed back to the central server. 
The central server gets the updated model weights from the 
chosen clients who have finished their local training during 
the model aggregation stage. Afterwards, the server 
integrates these updates using strategies like Federated 
Averaging (FedAvg). The quantity of data samples that each 
client supplied during local training is considered by FedAvg 
when calculating the weighted average of the updated model 
weights. Using a weighted average ensures that customers 
with more data will have a greater impact on the overall 
model. All participating clients can access the updated global 
model for the subsequent training session when the central 
server has combined the model changes. In the next cycle, 
clients will begin local training using this revised model as a 
guide. After numerous rounds of this iterative client selection 
process, local model training, and model aggregation, the 
global model converges and performs well on a validation 
dataset in Figure 4.
